Research design depends on the questions a project sets out to answer. The familiar divide between quantitative and qualitative frameworks often creates debate, but the decision is not a contest between preferences. Each methodology handles a different type of empirical problem.

## 1. The research question

Different questions require different forms of evidence:

- Questions like "how much?", "how often?", and "what is the probability?" require quantitative data. The focus is measuring magnitude, testing statistical correlations, modeling causality, and drawing representative samples.
- Questions like "why?", "how?", and "what does it mean?" call for qualitative methods. The goal is exploring mechanisms, motivations, institutional dynamics, and narratives that tables of numbers do not capture on their own.
- Questions addressing both the scale of a problem and how people perceive it benefit from mixed methods.

<Callout type="note" title="Methodological alignment">
  Fitting regression models to a question that requires in-depth interviews produces numbers without meaningful context. Similarly, drawing general conclusions about an entire population from a handful of interview quotes leads to weak inferences.
</Callout>

## 2. When to use quantitative methods

Quantitative research relies on standardized variables and sample control. It fits three main situations:

1. Population generalizations: when findings from a probability sample need to be projected onto a wider population with a calculated margin of error (such as 95% confidence intervals).
2. Hypothesis testing: when theory proposes causal relationships tested through linear, logistic, or panel data regressions.
3. Tracking over time: when a project monitors changes in the same indicators across months or years.

These methods are standard in economics, epidemiology, and comparative political science. In industry, they inform A/B tests and customer retention models. In government, they support quasi-experimental evaluations of public programs.

## 3. When to use qualitative methods

Qualitative research investigates context, language, and the reasoning behind decisions. Common applications include:

1. Emerging topics: when little literature exists and the study needs inductive fieldwork to identify starting hypotheses.
2. Narratives and perceptions: when the project centers on lived experiences, community priorities, or barriers to access.
3. Reconstructing decisions: when the goal is tracing the chain of events leading to a policy or institutional change (process tracing).

Software packages like NVivo, ATLAS.ti, MAXQDA, and QCAmap help organize transcripts and notes, making coding and category development systematic and auditable.

## 4. Mixed methods and triangulation

Combining quantitative and qualitative data allows researchers to examine questions from multiple angles. Three designs are common:

- Explanatory sequential (quantitative followed by qualitative): begins with statistical analysis of a survey or dataset, then uses follow-up interviews to explain outliers or causal mechanisms.
- Exploratory sequential (qualitative followed by quantitative): starts with interviews or focus groups to identify themes, which then inform a structured survey for a broader group.
- Convergent parallel: collects numerical data and fieldwork records concurrently, comparing both sets during analysis.

<Callout type="tip" title="Practical applications">
  In academia, this approach unpacks institutional mechanisms in dissertations and journal articles. In business, it pairs satisfaction metrics with focus groups to pinpoint why users leave. In government, it combines census indicators with local interviews to evaluate service delivery.
</Callout>

## Practical takeaway

Choosing tools before defining the core question usually creates rework. The clearest path is writing out the specific questions first, checking whether the needed data already exists or must be gathered, and selecting the techniques best suited to answer each question directly.
