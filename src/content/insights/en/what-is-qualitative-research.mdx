Qualitative research examines the meaning individuals and groups assign to social, cultural, or institutional experiences. Rather than counting frequencies or calculating statistical correlations, its purpose is to unpack subjective processes, implicit motives, everyday routines, and power relations.

This approach works with textual, audiovisual, and observational evidence. The researcher interprets discourse and uncovers thematic structures without detaching statements from the social setting where they took place.

## Collection methods: interviews, focus groups, and fieldwork

Researchers collect qualitative evidence through several fieldwork approaches:

- In-depth and semi-structured interviews: guided conversations based on open questions that allow informants to reflect on their careers, perspectives, or professional decisions.
- Focus groups: facilitated sessions with six to ten participants to map collective agreements, tensions, and group norms around a specific issue.
- Participant observation and ethnography: direct immersion in a community, government office, or corporate department, recording social dynamics through systematic field notes.
- Archival and document research: critical examination of public policy records, court filings, committee minutes, and institutional declarations.

## Analytical techniques: content, discourse, and grounded theory

Once interviews are transcribed and materials cataloged, researchers apply structured interpretive frameworks:

1. Content analysis: a classical three-stage technique comprising pre-analysis (exploratory reading and corpus boundaries), material exploration (coding recording units and building analytic categories), and result interpretation (drawing grounded inferences).
2. Discourse analysis: examines the ideological context, power dynamics, and competing meanings embedded in spoken or written language.
3. Grounded theory: an inductive methodology where theoretical categories emerge directly from continuous comparison across field observations.

## Validity criteria: saturation and triangulation

Unlike statistical inference, which targets population representation, qualitative studies seek explanatory depth. The validity of conclusions rests on two core principles:

- Theoretical saturation: fieldwork ends when new interviews or archival records no longer add novel insights to existing conceptual categories.
- Methodological and source triangulation: systematic cross-checking between multiple forms of evidence (such as corroborating interview claims with administrative records and direct field notes) to identify consistencies and contradictions.

## Qualitative software tools (CAQDAS)

Computer-assisted qualitative data analysis packages support organized coding across large collections of text, audio, and visual records:

- NVivo and ATLAS.ti: standard software packages for node hierarchies, conceptual network diagrams, query matrices, and inter-coder reliability checks (such as Cohen's Kappa).
- MAXQDA: widely adopted in social policy and mixed-methods research, offering seamless integration between qualitative codes and quantitative demographic tables.
- Iramuteq: an open-source R-based tool for lexicographic analysis, term clustering, and descending hierarchical classification of speech corpora.
